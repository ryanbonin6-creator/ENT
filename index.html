THE APPROACH

Grass is the goal. Evidence is the game plan. This project advocates for conversion through feasibility studies, independent evaluation, and player input—not a promise that every grass field is safer.

01 / THE WHY

The main problem
we are solving.

How can the NFL move every stadium toward high-quality natural grass while verifying safety, maintaining consistent field performance, and making the transition practical?

Put player welfare first.

Players rely on predictable traction and a surface that performs under changing weather, footwear, and game conditions. Artificial turf raises concerns about lower-extremity injuries and player confidence. Our goal is natural grass across the NFL, supported by site-specific engineering and credible outcome data.

Ryan Bonin’s report began with a concern about owners prioritizing costs over health. It correctly revises that assumption: motives cannot be established without budgets or interviews, and grass conversion is not a simple universal fix.

A material is not a safety guarantee.

Mack et al. reported a 16% higher lower-extremity injury rate per play on synthetic turf. McCormick et al. found no statistically significant difference for a narrower measure: season-ending injuries per game. Venishetty et al. also reported higher injury rates on turf. These studies measure different outcomes and include overlapping seasons. [A1–A3]

The responsible conclusion: investigate conversion, measure field quality, and avoid claiming that turf caused a particular injury or that grass eliminates risk.

A practical path to a grass-first league.

01

Check feasibility

Audit light, drainage, climate, storage, field recovery, and the event calendar. Obtain itemized engineering quotes.

02

Run a reviewed pilot

Choose a feasible venue and a comparison field. Agree on performance limits and a research protocol before launch.

03

Measure what matters

Track noncontact lower-extremity injuries, player exposure, days lost, field performance, costs, and player feedback.

04

Expand on evidence

Require sustained performance and adequate observations across seasons. Revise or stop if quality or operations fail.

State Farm Stadium’s movable grass tray demonstrates one engineering approach, not proof that other venues can afford or accommodate the same retrofit. [A6]

What goes into the estimate?

Each synthetic venue receives a provisional scenario: outdoor, retractable-roof, or enclosed. Upfront costs cover removal/site preparation, drainage and root zone, grass installation, equipment/lighting allowances, and a 25% contingency. Annual budgets include staffing and routine agronomy, water and utilities, and a resurfacing reserve. Existing-grass venues receive an annual operating allowance only.

The time-horizon total equals upfront cost plus annual cost × years, in constant planning dollars. It excludes inflation, financing, taxes, land acquisition, roof reconstruction, lost event revenue, and major stadium redevelopment; those costs are unknown and could be substantial. It is not a complete engineering estimate or an upper bound. No savings, injury reduction, or return on investment is assumed. [M1]

01 — The refined question

How can stadium operators transition toward higher-quality natural grass while verifying safety improvements and addressing the practical challenges? The goal remains player health. The report does not assume that every grass surface is safer or that owners’ motives have been established.

02 — Work completed

The project is a desk study of three original injury studies, league policy, NFLPA player-preference reporting, stadium operator documentation, and Warren Berger’s questioning framework. It compares injury definitions, exposure measures, time periods, and limitations. No player interviews, field experiments, or primary fieldwork have been completed.

03 — Evidence and its limits

Mack et al.: 2012–2016 mandated team reporting; synthetic turf had a 16% higher lower-extremity injury rate per play (IRR 1.16, 95% CI 1.10–1.23). Observational results do not establish causation or the performance of every current field. [A1]

McCormick et al.: 199 season-ending lower-extremity injuries across 891 games in 2020–2022; 0.242 per game on turf versus 0.199 on grass, without a statistically significant difference. That does not establish equivalent safety. [A2]

Venishetty et al.: 1.42 lower-extremity injuries per game on turf versus 1.22 on grass in 2021–2022; adjusted odds ratio for season-ending surgery 1.60 (95% CI 1.28–1.99). Public reports may miss injuries; practice and preseason were excluded. Odds are not probabilities, and seasons overlap the McCormick study. [A3]

Player preference: the NFLPA’s 2024 Titans report card reports that 92% of surveyed players preferred natural grass. This is a preference measure, not an injury rate or survey response rate. [A5]

04 — What changed

The report moves away from unverified claims about owner motives and an immediate universal fix. A movable grass field at State Farm Stadium demonstrates feasibility in one purpose-built venue, but existing stadiums may lack the same space or infrastructure. Small or nonsignificant findings do not prove equivalence, and overlapping datasets should not be counted as independent replications.

05 — Three alternatives

Immediate league-wide conversion: responds to player preferences but lacks comparable retrofit budgets and verified operating plans.

Phased conversion: prioritizes feasibility, independent evaluation, player feedback, and sustained outcomes before expansion. This is the report’s preferred direction.

Retain synthetic turf with monitoring: provides a comparator and potential fallback, while recognizing that better field test scores may not translate to fewer injuries or greater confidence.

06 — Decision gates

First obtain a site-specific plan and itemized estimates. Then seek expert review of injury definitions, exposure tracking, comparison groups, confounders, and sample size. Set field-performance and operating limits before a pilot. Expand only with sustained quality, transparent costs, player input, and enough outcome evidence. Zero injuries in a small sample is not proof of safety.

07 — Prioritized learning plan

Consult a professional grounds manager about light, drainage, recovery, storage, scheduling, and authority to approve change. Ask an athletic trainer or researcher to review injury measures and confounders such as footwear, prior injury, weather, position, and field age. Obtain separate estimates for installation, maintenance, resurfacing, lighting, drainage, and event disruption.

Seek consent for non-leading conversations with players who have used both surfaces, documenting the playing level and separating statements from interpretation. Verify policy implementation, field-failure procedures, study corrections, and disclosures. These are proposed next steps—not completed interviews or experiments.

08 — Framework, policy, and attribution

Berger’s “Why / What If / How” framework guides the project from understanding the problem to exploring alternatives and testing practical action. Its application here is the author’s interpretation. [A7]

The report cites a December 15, 2025 NFL announcement describing standards for grass and synthetic surfaces phased in during 2026–2028. Implementation and outcomes need separate verification; this website has not independently checked that policy. [A4]

The original report states that ChatGPT+ helped organize sources and the appendix, while the author retained responsibility for ideas, analysis, and decisions. Website cost scenarios are additional illustrative analysis and were not in the report.

M1 — Cost model and stadium reference inventory

Original Turf Burn scenario model, created for this website. No cost range is a sourced bid, vendor quote, verified stadium budget, or published industry benchmark. Treat every figure as a placeholder for feasibility discussions. The 2024 reference inventory is provisional and must be checked against current stadium operator records, including naming rights, surface replacements, and new Buffalo and Tennessee venues.

Outdoor turf conversion: $2.75–6.50M before 25% contingency; annual care plus reserve $0.70–1.50M. Retractable-roof turf conversion: $5.75–15.50M before contingency; annual $1.05–2.20M. Enclosed turf conversion: $12–35M before contingency; annual $1.55–3.50M. Existing grass: no conversion allowance; annual care plus reserve $0.60–1.40M. See each row for line items. These category-based assumptions are not a venue survey.

All 32 teams share 30 reference venues: the Giants/Jets and Rams/Chargers each share one. Existing reinforced or hybrid natural-grass surfaces are grouped with grass. Supplemental light, ventilation, drainage, event protection, and grass replacement need site-specific review. Unknown roof modifications and event-disruption costs are excluded, so ranges are not all-in upper bounds.
