    1. Refined Problem and Innovation Question

My original proposal asked whether the financial/aesthetic benefits of artificial turf outweigh players’
health. After refining it further, the investigation now points to a more specific problem: While accounting
for the conditions needed to maintain a safe grass field, how can the NFL reduce the avoidable surface-
related injury risks? NFL players bear the physical consequences, and their careers are affected while the
team and stadium owners manage field performance. My concern remains player health, but as I did prior,
I cannot assume owners’ motives or conclude that all grass surfaces are safer for players. Going forward,
my updated innovation question is: How can stadium operators make the transition towards higher quality
natural grass while verifying the safety improvements and addressing the problems that come with the
new change? This keeps my original goal intact while shifting from more of an accusation to a question
that can lead the way with proper comparisons and allow necessary testing.

    2. Work Completed and Evidence Gathered

This is a desk study of three original research studies on NFL injuries. I used the league’s policy, the
NFLPA report on players, and the stadium operator’s report [A1-A6]. I have compared the time periods of
the studies, the definition of injuries, the measurement of exposure, and limitations instead of counting
the number of articles in favor of grass. The original studies were helpful in assessing the injury claims,
whereas institutional sources clarified the policy and feasibility issues. Appendix A has documented the
evidence and its few limitations. This is secondary evidence, not primary (zero interviews or
conversations with players occurred).

    3. Insights, Patterns, and Surprises

One of the patterns that I learned is that studies can reach different conclusions simply because they
measure different types of injuries. This was shown as Mack et al. found a 16% higher rate of lower-body
injuries per play on artificial turf [A1], while McCormick et al. found no statistically significant
difference in season-ending injuries per game [A2]. Venishetty et al. also found higher injury rates and
higher odds of season-ending surgery on turf [A3]. Although they all cover injuries, they cover different
seasons and are measuring the injuries much differently. That means their results cannot be compared
directly. This gives me a reason to investigate grass conversion further without just assuming it would
make every field safer, which was a surprise to me as I thought grass was just safer. I learned that players’
preferences matter even when the safest playing surface is unknown. Players’ strong preference for grass
shows that they have concerns that deserve more attention [A5]. Berger’s usage of the “Why / What If /
How” framework helps me turn those concerns into a practical next step [A7]. This leads me to wonder
yet again: Why do players remain concerned when their preferences are just preferences? Another one I
wonder is: What if one stadium tested a transition to grass? After this, could its safety, maintenance costs,
and event schedule be evaluated and investigated for other teams?



    4. Assumptions and Changes in My Thinking

I started a project by assuming turf mainly reflected owners choosing their own wealth over the players’
health. Now, that remains a most certainly unverified explanation; I did not use stadium budgets or
decision-maker interviews at first, and I also treated grass conversion as a very simple and straightforward

fix for this whole problem. State Farm Stadium’s movable field concept demonstrates a workable design

    5. Alternatives and Current Direction

Three alternative directions I compared are: immediate league-wide grass conversion, the slow
implementation of grass conversion with data, and continued synthetic use with better monitoring. The
immediate conversion plan directly addresses player preferences [A5] but lacks a verified operating plan.
Monitoring this is also an important comparison option, because the material alone does not relate to
performance while leaving player concerns unresolved. The slow implementation idea is strong at this
stage, because it makes it testable before applying it to many stadiums and scaling it to the league. This
allows independent reviews and player input, and it would track noncontact lower extremity injuries per
player, how many days they were out, field performance, and ultimately all player feedback. Next,
Berger’s “How” stage requires specifying what would change my mind [A7]: inconsistent grass
performance or unacceptable operation would trigger a new approach; also, having better sustained
outcomes would support expansion. This would be a proposed evaluation, not a completed test. Appendix
B compares the alternatives and defines decision gates more thoroughly with data.

    6. Remaining Questions, Risks, and Next Steps

Remaining questions and gaps in the ideas I have still are simply the absence of comparable stadium
costs, along with uncertainty about injury observations and their differences post-conversion. For the fat
portion of this portfolio, I will attempt to seek a grounds manager’s explanation of a natural grass surface.
He/she plane, crash, recovery, light, drainage, and the effect on other event scheduling at stadiums. I
would also go down the route of talking to an athletic trainer or sports medicine researcher to talk about
injury severity on each surface, as well as player recovery and confidence after injuries. I will be able to
obtain certain operation estimates for this process and develop certain scenarios for each stadium. A single
season after this conversion would be way too small of a sample to investigate injury reduction
statistically, so, after a few years, a researcher should assess that sample size and investigate before they
make any conclusions on this controversial topic. Appendix C specifies evidence needed to decide
whether to advance, revise, or reject the idea I am putting out there.

Appendix A Evidence Register

Research reviewed October 16, 2026. Labels A1–A7 link report claims to sources. Facts summarize
published material; interpretations and proposed actions are identified separately. No primary fieldwork
has been completed for this report.

A1 Higher injury rate in a large NFL cohort

Mack and colleagues analyzed 2012–2016 regular-season injuries using mandated reporting from all 32
teams. Synthetic turf had a 16% higher lower-extremity injury rate per play (IRR 1.16; 95% CI 1.10–
1.23).

Source: Mack et al. (2019), American Journal of Sports Medicine, 47(1), 189–196.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30452873/

Limit and project meaning: Observational association; historical fields and seasons cannot establish the
risk of every current product. Supports investigating grass conversion, not attributing a particular player’s
injury to turf.

A2 A different outcome produces a less certain result

McCormick and colleagues found 199 season-ending lower-extremity injuries across 891 NFL games in
2020–2022. Rates were 0.242 per game on artificial turf and 0.199 on grass; the difference was not
statistically significant.

Source: McCormick et al. (2024), Translational Sports Medicine, article 6832213.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39553464/

Limit and project meaning: Publicly available records and a narrow outcome. Failure to detect
significance does not establish equivalence. Supports separating injury definitions and exposure
measures.

A3 Recent research also raises concern

Venishetty and colleagues reported 1.42 lower-extremity injuries per game on artificial turf versus 1.22 on
grass in 2021–2022. Their adjusted odds ratio for season-ending surgery was 1.60 (95% CI 1.28–1.99).

Source: Venishetty et al. (2024), Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine, 12(8).
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23259671241265378

Limit and project meaning: Public game reports may miss injuries; practice and preseason injuries were
excluded. Odds are not probabilities. The seasons overlap A2, so these are not independent replications.

Appendix A Institutional and Course Evidence

A4 Standards are changing

NFL standards permit grass and synthetic fields, with compliance phased in during 2026–2028.

Source: NFL (December 15, 2025), Safety and Consistency in Field Surfaces.
https://www.nfl.com/playerhealthandsafety/equipment-and-innovation/engineering-technology/safety-
and-consistency-in-field-surfaces-nfl-and-nflpa-mandatory-practices

Limit and project meaning: A league policy announcement establishes requirements, not demonstrated
injury reductions. Verify implementation and outcomes separately.

A5 Player preference is strong

The NFLPA’s 2024 Titans report card states that 92% of surveyed players across the league preferred
natural grass to synthetic surfaces.

Source: NFLPA (2024), Tennessee Titans Report Card.
https://nflpa.com/tennessee-titans-report-card-2024

Limit and project meaning: A union-reported preference measure, not a clinical injury rate. The indexed
source text was available; full-page access was blocked. Do not describe 92% as the survey response rate.

Source: State Farm Stadium, About, accessed October 16, 2026.

Limit and project meaning: Operator documentation establishes a technical example, not the affordability

Appendix B Alternatives and Decision Logic

This comparison is analytical work completed for the report. It is not evidence that a stadium approved an
option. Costs and injury benefits remain unverified.

Immediate conversion at every venue

Potential value: directly responds to the preference documented in A5. Weak point: no comparable retrofit
budgets, recovery plans, or venue-specific performance data. Decision: do not recommend a universal
deadline on the present record.

Phased conversion at a feasible venue

Potential value: tests grass quality and operating feasibility before expansion. A6 demonstrates one
engineering approach; A1–A3 justify evaluating injury outcomes. Weak point: selection bias,
implementation expense, and insufficient injury observations. Decision: prioritize a feasibility study
before proposing a live pilot.

Retain synthetic fields with performance monitoring

Potential value: offers a comparison against conversion without assuming a material label determines
safety. Weak point: improved test results may not translate into fewer injuries or greater player
confidence. Decision: retain as a comparator and possible fallback, contingent on independently assessed
outcomes.

Proposed decision gates

1. Feasibility: obtain a site-specific plan and itemized estimates. Revise or reject the venue if adequate
grass cannot be sustained through its event calendar.
2. Evaluation: obtain expert review of injury definitions, exposure tracking, sample size, comparison
selection, and confounders before testing.
3. Continuation: agree on field-performance and operating limits before launch; investigate failures
promptly. Do not interpret zero injuries in a small sample as proof of safety.
4. Expansion: require sustained performance, transparent costs, player input, and enough outcome
evidence to justify wider use.

Appendix C Prioritized Learning Plan

The actions below are future work. No outreach, interview, quote, or experiment is represented as already
completed.

First resolve operating feasibility

Contact a stadium or professional grounds manager. Ask which light, drainage, recovery, storage, and
event-calendar constraints would prevent conversion. Request a sample maintenance schedule and
identify the decision-maker who could authorize a change. Output: a venue constraint checklist with
documented answers.

Then establish a credible safety comparison

Ask an athletic trainer or injury researcher which injury definitions and denominators are defensible.
Request guidance on prior injury, footwear, weather, position, field age, and schedule as confounders.
Output: a reviewed study-comparison sheet and proposed evaluation protocol, including whether multiple
seasons or venues are needed.

Test the financial assumption

Request separate installation, maintenance, resurfacing, lighting, drainage, and event-disruption estimates.
Use the same time horizon for both materials and disclose missing values. Output: a sensitivity analysis;
no claimed return on investment without credible inputs. Keep player welfare explicit rather than
reducing it to payroll savings.

Understand the user need directly

Seek consent for short conversations with players who have used both materials. Ask about traction,
recovery, confidence, and field-condition differences without leading them toward grass. Identify the
playing level and avoid generalizing local experiences to NFL players. Output: dated notes separating
participant statements from interpretation.

Close the policy and evidence gaps

Verify the policy text and available implementation records, including how field failures are handled.
Recheck study corrections and disclosures. Output: an updated source register and a decision explaining
whether the pilot should advance, change, or stop.
